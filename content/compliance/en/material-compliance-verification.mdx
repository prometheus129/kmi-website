A certificate of compliance certifies the **sample** a laboratory tested — not the goods in your container. The US importer of record, not the factory, holds the legal liability for what actually ships. Material compliance verification closes that gap: it confirms the material in your shipment is what the certificate claims, before a regulator or a customs hold finds out it isn't.

*Last verified June 14, 2026 against the eCFR, CPSC, and FDA primary sources cited below. CertDesk re-verifies this page quarterly.*

Most import-compliance advice treats the certificate as the finish line. You collected the supplier's test report, the numbers came in under the limits, you filed it. The problem is that a passing report speaks for one physical specimen on one date — and the most expensive compliance failures don't come from suppliers who never tested. They come from suppliers whose report was genuine, whose numbers really were compliant on the day of the test, and whose material then changed underneath the paperwork.

## What does a certificate of compliance actually certify?

A certificate certifies a sample. Under federal product-safety law, a General Certificate of Conformity (for general-use products) or a Children's Product Certificate (for children's products) attests that a representative sample of the product was tested and passed the applicable rules. It is a statement about evidence in hand, not a guarantee that every later production lot is identical.

That distinction is where compliance risk lives. The certificate and the shipment are two different things, joined only by the assumption that the material never changed. Three events break that link, and none of them appear in the document: regrind blended in after certification, a substituted resin, and a forged or mismatched test report. Each is covered below.

The legal weight of the certificate sits on the importer regardless. That is the single most misunderstood fact in import compliance, and it is worth stating plainly before going further.

## Who is liable — the factory or the importer of record?

The US importer of record is liable. The Consumer Product Safety Act defines a "manufacturer" to include importers — 15 USC 2052(a)(11) states that "the term 'manufacturer' means any person who manufactures or imports a consumer product." Section 14(a) of the Act (15 USC 2063) then requires that manufacturer to certify the product, and the CPSC's certification rule is unambiguous about who that is for an import:

> "Except as otherwise provided in a specific standard, in the case of a product manufactured outside the United States, only the importer must certify in accordance with, and provide the certificate required by, CPSA section 14(a)." — 16 CFR 1110.7

Read that against how testing actually works. The Chinese factory runs the test and hands you the report. But the importer is the entity the law names as the certifier, and the importer is the entity the CPSC pursues when a product fails. The factory's test does not transfer the liability — it only gives the importer something to certify from.

The numbers make the stakes concrete. Since January 1, 2022, the CPSC's maximum civil penalty has been **$120,000 per violation**, up to **$17,150,000** for a related series of violations. (The Commission adjusts these figures for inflation every five years, not annually — so they hold until the next scheduled adjustment, rather than drifting each year.) Recent penalties show the order of magnitude: $19.065 million against Peloton and $12.25 million against Fitbit. Both of those were failure-to-report cases rather than certificate-mismatch cases — but they establish that eight-figure exposure is real, not theoretical, for a US company that gets compliance wrong on imported goods.

## Which three regimes make the material the compliance risk?

For three categories of imported goods, the regulated thing is the material itself — what the polymer is, what was added to it, and what migrates or leaches out of it. A design change can fix a sharp edge or a tip-over hazard. Only the material answers a lead limit, a food-contact clearance, or a PFAS ban.

| Regime | What is regulated | Key thresholds | Where verification matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CPSIA / children's products** | Lead, phthalates, mechanical safety | Lead 100 ppm; eight phthalates above 0.1%; ASTM F963-23 mandatory since April 20, 2024 | A regrind or resin swap can breach the 100 ppm lead limit invisibly |
| **FDA food-contact** | Polymer + additives in contact with food | Material must meet the applicable clearance in 21 CFR Parts 174–178 (177 covers polymers) | "Food grade" is not an FDA approval; compliance rests on the specific resin and additives |
| **PFAS / state chemical bans** | Intentionally added PFAS and other restricted substances | State-by-state deadlines through 2026–2028 | The restricted substance is in the material or its coating, not the design |

**Children's products (CPSIA).** Lead content above 100 ppm and any of eight regulated phthalates above 0.1% will fail a children's product. Toys must meet ASTM F963-23, which has been the mandatory federal toy-safety standard since April 20, 2024. Certification requires testing by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory and a Children's Product Certificate. This is also where the cleanest legal definition of "the material changed" lives — covered in [does regrind or material substitution void your certificate?](/compliance/does-regrind-void-your-certification).

**FDA food-contact.** Food-contact materials are cleared under 21 CFR Parts 174 through 178, with Part 177 covering polymers specifically. Crucially, "food grade" and "food safe" are not FDA regulatory categories — there is no FDA definition of either term in those parts. A material is compliant when it meets the conditions of the applicable clearance, documented through a Declaration of Compliance backed by data — or, for imports, through a guaranty whose protections are narrower than most importers assume. That trap is dissected in [does an FDA Letter of Guaranty actually protect you?](/compliance/fda-letter-of-guaranty-protection).

**PFAS and state chemical bans.** A growing state patchwork restricts intentionally added PFAS and other substances in specific product categories. Maine's law (Title 38, section 1614) restricts intentionally added PFAS across a phased set of product categories — including cookware and textiles — with obligations from January 1, 2026. Minnesota's Amara's Law (Minnesota Statutes section 116.943) covers eleven categories, with manufacturer reporting due September 15, 2026. California's AB 1817 targets PFAS in textiles. Washington's Safer Products program carries its own reporting from January 1, 2026.

Two corrections matter here, because the field is full of stale claims. California's SB 682 cookware ban was **vetoed** by Governor Newsom on October 13, 2025 — it is not law, and any guide treating it as current is wrong. New York's PFAS cookware bill is **not yet in force**; New York's live PFAS ban applies to apparel and textiles. Because PFAS law moves month to month, the live state map and its deadlines live on a separately maintained page: [PFAS state requirements map for sellers, 2026](/compliance/pfas-state-requirements-map-sellers-2026).

## How does a genuine certificate end up on a non-compliant material?

Three mechanisms break the link between a passing certificate and the goods in your container, and none of them leave a mark on the document.

**Regrind added after certification.** A supplier tests virgin resin, earns a clean report, then blends reclaimed or regrind material into later production runs to cut cost. The certificate still reads "compliant." The regrind can carry heavy metals or unintended additives the original virgin sample never had — which is exactly how a product that passed at 100 ppm lead drifts over the line on a later lot. Internal factory regrind is effectively free, because it is ground scrap fed back into the melt — so the incentive to do it quietly is structural, not occasional. The full mechanism is in [does regrind void your certificate?](/compliance/does-regrind-void-your-certification).

**Resin substitution.** The grade you specified and the grade that was molded are not the same. A cheaper resin, a different supplier's "equivalent," or an off-spec lot gets substituted while your technical data sheet and your certificate keep describing the material you thought you bought.

**Forged or mismatched test reports.** Fake and altered reports are not a hypothetical. In January 2026, the CPSC withdrew its acceptance of four China-based testing laboratories after identifying falsified test results; reports those labs issued are no longer valid, and importers relying on them had to retest. Months later, on May 6, 2026, the Commission announced a separate crackdown on counterfeit certification marks, noting that many such fakes originate out of the People's Republic of China. The hardest version to catch is not an obviously fake number — it is a **genuine report number attached to the wrong product**. A free report-number lookup confirms the number is real; it cannot tell you the report describes your material. The verification workflow for that is in [how to verify a supplier's test report](/compliance/how-to-verify-supplier-test-report) and [fake test report red flags](/compliance/fake-test-report-red-flags); you can screen the issuing lab itself with the free [Lab & Test-Report Status Checker](/compliance/lab-status-checker).

In every case the importer of record carries the liability, and in every case the failure is in the material — not the paperwork.

## What can paperwork not prove, and what closes the gap?

A test report and a technical data sheet describe two different things, and neither one describes your shipment. The report describes the sample the lab received — one specimen, one date. The TDS describes the grade the producer sells — a product-line description, not evidence about any particular container. The only thing that verifies the material in your shipment is testing the material in your shipment.

That is material work, not document work. Confirming polymer identity, reading a regrind signature in a thermal trace, judging whether a filler loading is plausible for a given grade — a visual inspection cannot do it, and a company-registry check cannot do it. A pre-shipment inspector confirms the product looks right and the count is correct. A supplier-verification vendor confirms the company is a real legal entity. Neither identifies the material — and honesty about a material also depends on who reads it: a verifier that also sells you the goods has an incentive to find it acceptable, so the most reliable reading comes from a party with nothing to ship. The methods that do the work — FTIR for polymer identity, DSC and melt flow rate for grade consistency, ash and density for fillers — are laid out in [verifying resin and material against the TDS](/compliance/resin-material-verification-tds).

Two principles make this affordable and fast.

**Screen cheap at origin, then certify only what is ready.** A diligence-grade material screen is low-cost and can be run at origin, in China, before your shipment moves — so you catch a non-compliant material while it can still be fixed at the factory, not after your entry is held at a US port. The screen is not a substitute for an accredited certificate where the law requires one: a Children's Product Certificate must rest on a CPSC-accepted, ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory result — which CertDesk routes rather than issues ([how we verify](/compliance/how-we-verify)). But the screen tells you whether it is worth paying for that accredited test at all. There is no sense spending on a certification test for a material a cheaper screen would have shown is non-compliant.

**Verify per material, not per SKU.** Under the CPSC component-part rule (16 CFR 1109), a material or chemical test result attaches to the material and supplier and can be relied on across every SKU built from that identical material. One screen of a given resin from a given supplier covers all the products molded from it. You re-screen when a material or supplier changes — which is precisely the moment a "compliant" supplier might quietly swap in regrind, so it is the moment worth watching.

## A verification workflow for your next shipment

1. **Treat the certificate as a starting point, not proof.** Match the report to the specific material, grade, and supplier on your current production run — not to last year's sample.
2. **Verify the report is genuine and about your product.** Confirm the issuing lab is real and accredited, the report covers your SKU and the right standard, and — for children's products — the lab's CPSC acceptance covers the specific rule. See [how to verify a supplier's test report](/compliance/how-to-verify-supplier-test-report).
3. **Screen the material at origin before it ships.** Polymer identity, regrind and recycled-content signals, and filler loading, read against the TDS — the cheapest place and the last moment to fix a problem at the factory.
4. **Route accredited testing only when the law requires it** — for a Children's Product Certificate or any regulatory submission — and only on material your screen has already cleared.
5. **Re-verify on every material or supplier change.** A new lot from a "compliant" supplier is a new material until you have confirmed it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a certificate of compliance cover my whole shipment?

No. A certificate is backed by a test of one sample on one date. It is not evidence that every production lot in your containers is identical to that sample. When a supplier adds regrind, substitutes a resin, or switches material suppliers after the certificate is issued, the document stays valid on paper while the material underneath it no longer matches.

### Who is liable if an imported product fails a compliance test — the factory or the importer?

The US importer of record. Under section 14(a) of the Consumer Product Safety Act, the importer is the entity that must certify the product, and 16 CFR 1110.7 states that only the importer must certify an imported product. The factory runs the test, but the importer is the legal certifier and carries the federal liability — up to $120,000 per violation.

### What is the difference between regulatory-grade and diligence-grade material verification?

Regulatory-grade testing comes from a CPSC-accepted, ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory and can ground a Children's Product Certificate. Diligence-grade screening — polymer identity, regrind and filler checks against the TDS — tells you whether the material matches its certificate and is appropriate for your own due diligence and general-use products. The cost-efficient workflow is to screen cheaply first, then pay for the accredited test only on material you have confirmed is ready.

### Do I need to test every SKU separately?

For the material itself, usually no. Under the CPSC component-part rule (16 CFR 1109), a material or chemical test result attaches to the material and supplier and can be relied on across every SKU built from that identical material. A brand with 80 SKUs across 6 resins verifies 6 materials, not 80 products. Assembled-product tests — mechanical, small parts, ASTM F963 — are separate and product-specific.

### Is 'food grade' an FDA approval?

No. 'Food grade' and 'food safe' are not FDA regulatory categories — there is no FDA definition of either term in the food-contact rules at 21 CFR Parts 174 to 178. A material is FDA-compliant for food contact when it meets the specific conditions of the applicable clearance, documented through a Declaration of Compliance backed by data — not when a supplier labels it 'food grade.'
