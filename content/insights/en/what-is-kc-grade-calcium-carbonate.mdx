> **In short:** "KC grade" calcium carbonate is a **supplier's product code, not an industry standard.** A "KC-4" from one producer is not the same as another's — the letters usually abbreviate the producer, and the number tracks fineness or sequence. Compare grades by the actual spec — **top cut (D97), whiteness with its measurement standard, purity, and coating** — not by the code. In the **Kantor KC Series**, the number tracks the top cut, from KC-4 (≈5.5 µm, finest) to KC-17 (≈17 µm), with the same chemistry across the whole ladder.

## "KC" is a product code, not a specification

Calcium carbonate producers name their grades with short codes: a letter or two that abbreviates the company or the product line, and a number for fineness or sequence. "KC" is one such prefix, and more than one producer uses it. There is **no ISO, ASTM, or national standard that defines a "KC grade"** of ground calcium carbonate.

That has a practical consequence. Two grades both labelled "KC-4" can differ in particle size, whiteness, purity, and coating, because each producer sets its own numbering. The code is a label, not a promise — so a grade code alone is never enough to compare or qualify a filler.

## Read the spec, not the code

Four numbers actually decide a GCC grade for plastics. Line these up across suppliers and the code stops mattering:

1. **Top cut (D97) — the primary selector.** The size below which 97% of the particles fall. Match it to your product's gauge: defects in film and other thin or surface-critical products come from the coarse tail of the distribution, so the D97 predicts performance better than the average size (D50).
2. **Whiteness and its measurement standard.** A whiteness figure is only comparable if you know how it was measured. **ISO brightness (R457 / ISO 2470)** is the reference method; a bare "whiteness %" with no stated standard cannot be compared across suppliers. Ask for both the number and the method — see [whiteness vs. ISO brightness](/insights/calcium-carbonate-whiteness-vs-iso-brightness).
3. **Purity and iron.** The CaCO₃ content (higher is better) and the Fe₂O₃ level (lower is better — iron dulls whiteness and can drift lot to lot).
4. **Coating.** Coated (stearic acid) or uncoated — an orthogonal choice driven by loading and your dispersion process, not by the grade number. See [coated vs. uncoated](/insights/coated-vs-uncoated-calcium-carbonate-when-to-use-which).

## Mesh or micron?

Some producers code by mesh, others by micron, which is another way the same grade can look like two different products. **Mesh** counts the openings per inch of a sieve, so a higher mesh number is finer. **Micron** (D50 / D97) measures the particle size directly by laser diffraction (ISO 13320), so a lower micron number is finer.

Higher mesh and lower micron both mean finer, but the two scales are not exactly interchangeable — mesh describes a sieve, microns describe the particles. As a rough guide, **1250 mesh is around 10 µm.** Where a supplier gives only a mesh number, ask for the D97 in microns so you can compare like for like.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is 'KC grade' calcium carbonate an industry standard?**

No — "KC" is a supplier's product code, not a standardized designation. No ISO, ASTM, or national standard defines a "KC grade." Different producers use KC-numbered grades to mean different things, so compare by the actual spec (D97, whiteness and its method, purity, coating), not the code.

**Does KC-4 mean the same thing from every supplier?**

No. Without a common definition, one producer's KC-4 can differ in particle size, whiteness, purity, and coating from another's. Read the technical data sheet: match the D97 to your gauge and check whiteness (with its standard, e.g. R457 / ISO 2470), CaCO₃ content, and Fe₂O₃.

**What do the Kantor KC Series numbers mean?**

"KC" is the Kantor line and the number tracks the top cut (D97): KC-4 ≈ 5.5 µm (finest) to KC-17 ≈ 17 µm (coarsest). Chemistry and whiteness are the same across the ladder — only fineness changes.

**Is calcium carbonate graded by mesh or micron?**

Both, depending on the producer. Mesh counts sieve openings per inch; micron (D50 / D97) measures particle size by laser diffraction. Higher mesh and lower micron both mean finer. As a rough guide, 1250 mesh is around 10 µm — but the scales are not exactly convertible, so compare the D97 where you can.

**How do I compare two calcium carbonate grades with different codes?**

Ignore the codes and line up four numbers: top cut (D97) versus your gauge, whiteness and its measurement standard, CaCO₃ purity and Fe₂O₃, and coated versus uncoated. A code tells you nothing a spec sheet does not.
