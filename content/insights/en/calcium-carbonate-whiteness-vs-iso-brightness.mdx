> **In short:** Whiteness and ISO brightness measure different things. **Whiteness** is a broad "how white across the whole visible spectrum" score that can be measured several ways — so an unqualified "whiteness 98" is not comparable between suppliers unless you know the method. **ISO brightness (R457 / ISO 2470)** is a single, standardized number — blue-light reflectance at 457 nm — that *is* comparable. When you compare GCC grades, compare the ISO brightness and ask the method. Kantor's KC Series is whiteness ≥98 **and** ISO brightness ≥95 (R457 / ISO 2470), stated together.

## Whiteness and brightness are not the same measurement

Buyers use "whiteness" and "brightness" interchangeably, but for calcium carbonate they are two different numbers:

- **Whiteness** describes how white and neutral a powder looks across the *whole* visible spectrum. It can be measured on more than one scale — a Hunter whiteness, a CIE whiteness index, the Chinese-standard (GB/T) whiteness that most Chinese GCC grades are quoted on, and others — and the figure depends on which method was used.
- **Brightness**, in the mineral and paper world, means **ISO brightness (ISO 2470)** — the reflectance of a single blue wavelength, **457 nm**, which is why it is often written **R457**. It is one number, defined by one method.

The practical consequence: two suppliers can both say "whiteness 98" and mean different things, because they measured on different scales. Two suppliers who both quote "ISO brightness 95 (R457)" are measuring the same way.

| **Most sensitive to** | General appearance | Yellowing and impurity (iron, organics) |
| **How GCC typically reads** | A few points higher | A few points lower — the stricter number |

## Why the blue wavelength?

The impurities that make calcium carbonate look off-white — iron and trace organics — show up as **yellowing**, and yellow absorbs blue light. So a **blue-light** reflectance number is a sensitive, standardized single measure of how clean the powder actually is. A high R457 means little yellow and low impurity; a grade can look "white" to the eye and still score lower on R457 if it carries a yellow cast. That sensitivity is why the paper and mineral-filler industries standardized on 457 nm as the comparable brightness number — and why filler suppliers quote it into coatings and plastics.

## Why ISO brightness is the number to compare on

If you are choosing between grades, the ISO brightness is the figure that lets you compare like with like:

- **Unqualified "whiteness" is the softer claim.** It is the number that reads well on a spec sheet, but without the method it is not comparable, and the same powder can score several points higher on a whiteness index than it does on ISO brightness.
- **ISO brightness (R457 / ISO 2470) is the rigorous claim.** Method-defined, so it travels: the same powder gives the same number in your lab and the supplier's.

So when a grade is offered as "whiteness 98," the useful next question is: *measured how, and what is the ISO brightness?* Comparing an unstated-method whiteness against a named ISO brightness is comparing two different rulers.
