## The corridor math has changed

Before March 2026, Southeast Asian polymer buyers sourced from four supply corridors: the Middle East (via Hormuz), Korea, Singapore/Indonesia, and China. Each corridor had different economics, different risk profiles, and different product specializations.

The Strait of Hormuz closure didn't just disrupt one corridor. It triggered a cascade that has effectively narrowed four corridors to one.

## What happened — and why it compounds

The direct impact is straightforward: Middle Eastern polyolefin exports — SABIC, Borouge, QAPCO, and others — transit through Hormuz. With the strait closed, those volumes are physically unavailable to Asian buyers regardless of price.

The indirect impacts are where procurement teams need to focus:

**Korea** — YNCC has suspended naphtha cracker operations. Korea imports virtually all of its naphtha, and a significant share transits the Strait of Hormuz or originates from Middle Eastern suppliers (Korea also sources naphtha from Australia, Southeast Asia, and domestic refining, but not enough to offset a Hormuz closure). When imported naphtha becomes scarce, crackers shut down, and Korea's PP and PE exports to Southeast Asia dry up.

**Singapore and Indonesia** — A cascade of force majeures: PCS (Singapore), TPC, and Chandra Asri have all declared force majeure or reduced operations. These facilities depend on naphtha feedstock that has become structurally more expensive since the closure.

**China** — The only corridor still operating at near-normal capacity. China's petrochemical complex has structural advantages: significant coal-to-olefins (CTO) and methanol-to-olefins (MTO) capacity that doesn't depend on naphtha imports. Combined with a large strategic petroleum reserve and commercial inventory, China has the deepest buffer of any producing region.

## Corridor-by-corridor breakdown

Understanding where polymer supply stands requires examining each producing region's exposure to the Hormuz disruption individually. The situation is not uniform — some corridors are fully blocked, others are degraded, and one remains structurally insulated.

### Middle East: fully blocked

The Middle East accounts for a significant share of global polyolefin exports, and the vast majority of those volumes transit the Strait of Hormuz. SABIC (Jubail and Yanbu), Borouge (Ruwais, Abu Dhabi), QAPCO (Qatar), and EQUATE (Kuwait) all rely on the strait for eastbound shipments to Asia. With the strait closed, these producers face two options: redirect volumes westward through the Red Sea and Suez Canal (adding cost and time), or halt exports entirely. Neither option helps Southeast Asian buyers in the near term.

For buyers who historically relied on Middle Eastern PE grades — particularly metallocene LLDPE from Borouge or HDPE pipe grades from SABIC — the supply gap is immediate and has no short-term workaround within the same corridor. These are not interchangeable with Korean or Chinese grades without requalification.

Yanbu-based capacity (some SABIC lines, Natpet for PP) can technically bypass Hormuz via the Red Sea. However, Natpet produces PP only and has zero PE capacity. Yanbu volumes that can reach Asia via Suez face longer transit times and elevated freight costs, making them uncompetitive against China-origin supply for most SE Asian buyers.

### Korea and Japan: naphtha-dependent, degraded

Korea is the second-largest polymer supplier to Southeast Asia by value. Korean producers — LG Chem, Lotte Chemical, Hanwha Total, YNCC — operate naphtha-based crackers almost exclusively. Korea imports approximately 90% of its naphtha requirements. A substantial portion of that supply originates from the Middle East or transits Hormuz.

With naphtha supply constrained, Korean crackers face a binary choice: secure alternative naphtha at elevated prices (spot naphtha from non-Hormuz sources is available but at a premium) or reduce operating rates. YNCC has already suspended operations. Other Korean producers are running at reduced rates and prioritizing domestic supply commitments over export allocations.

Japan's situation is similar in structure but different in impact for SE Asian buyers. Japan holds larger strategic reserves (approximately 200 days combined strategic and commercial), but Japan is not a major polymer exporter to Southeast Asia. Japanese production is consumed domestically. The relevance for SE Asian buyers is indirect: as Korean supply tightens, Japanese domestic buyers do not absorb Korean overflow, meaning Korean export cuts translate directly to reduced availability in Vietnam and the Philippines.

### India: Jamnagar exposed, mixed picture

India's largest refining and petrochemical complex — Reliance Industries at Jamnagar, Gujarat — processes crude oil that transits the Arabian Sea and, for many grades, originates from Hormuz-dependent Middle Eastern producers. While India has diversified its crude sources (Russia has become a larger supplier), the disruption to Middle Eastern crude flows still tightens feedstock economics for Indian crackers.

Indian polymer exports to Southeast Asia are modest compared to Middle Eastern, Korean, or Chinese volumes, but for buyers sourcing specific Indian grades (particularly certain PP copolymers), this corridor is degraded rather than blocked. Expect Indian export allocations to tighten and prices to rise as Jamnagar-adjacent facilities compete for non-Hormuz crude.

### China: structurally insulated, the last corridor

China's petrochemical complex operates on a fundamentally different feedstock architecture than every other producing region. While coastal crackers (Zhenhai, Fujian, Dalian) use imported naphtha and face the same feedstock squeeze as Korean and Singaporean facilities, China has a second production pathway: coal-to-olefins (CTO) and propane dehydrogenation (PDH) units that operate on domestic coal or imported propane (mostly from the US and Australia, neither of which transits Hormuz).

CTO and PDH capacity provides a production floor that no other exporting region possesses. This capacity is concentrated inland (Shaanxi, Inner Mongolia, Ningxia for CTO; Zhejiang and Shandong for PDH) and produces standard PP and PE grades suitable for most commodity applications.

Additionally, China holds approximately 80-90 days of strategic petroleum reserves with a government target of 120 days, plus substantial commercial inventories. Even for naphtha-based coastal crackers, China has a deeper buffer before forced shutdowns than any other Asian producer.

## Freight impact: surcharges, insurance, and transit times

The Hormuz closure has triggered a chain reaction in freight markets that affects all corridors — including China-to-SE Asia routes that don't transit the strait.

**War Risk Insurance (WRI):** Vessels transiting the Arabian Sea or approaching the Gulf of Oman face elevated WRI premiums. These premiums are being passed through to shippers as surcharges. For routes that transit near the affected zone, WRI adds an estimated $15-30 per TEU on top of base freight.

**Emergency fuel surcharges:** CMA CGM has already imposed an Emergency Fuel Surcharge of $75/TEU on intra-Asia routes. Other carriers are expected to follow. The rationale is elevated bunker fuel prices driven by crude supply tightening — even though intra-Asia routes don't transit Hormuz, bunker prices are global.

**Equipment repositioning:** Container equipment imbalances are worsening. Middle Eastern export volumes that previously returned empty containers to Asia are no longer flowing. This tightens container availability at Chinese load ports, particularly for 40ft high-cube containers. Expect equipment surcharges to increase through Q2 2026.

**China-to-Vietnam rates (current baseline):** Ningbo to HCMC remains $550/40HQ ocean freight (approximately $38/MT all-in at ~22 MT loading). Shanghai to HCMC is $200-400/40ft. Guangzhou/Nansha to HCMC is the shortest route at 2-5 days transit and $150-300/40ft. These rates are pre-escalation — budget for 15-25% increases through Q2 if the closure persists.

**China-to-Philippines rates:** Manila FCL rates entered Q1 2026 at multi-year lows ($125/20GP, $175/40GP in February). These rates will face upward pressure from the same surcharge and equipment dynamics, but the starting point is lower than Vietnam routes.

**Transit times are stable for China-SE Asia routes.** Shanghai to HCMC remains 7-10 days, Ningbo to HCMC 7-10 days, Qingdao to HCMC 7-10 days. The Hormuz closure does not directly affect these routes. However, if carriers reroute Middle East-bound vessels to alternative trade lanes, schedule reliability on intra-Asia routes may deteriorate.

## The first filter is now geography, not price

For procurement teams evaluating polymer supply, the decision framework has inverted. Previously, the first question was "what's the best price?" Now the first question is "which corridor is actually shipping?"

This changes how buyers should evaluate quotes:

- A competitive quote from a Korean trader is meaningless if the producer can't get naphtha
- Middle Eastern FOB prices are irrelevant when vessels can't transit
- China-origin pricing should be evaluated against availability, not against prices that exist only on paper

## Timeline: what to expect if the strait remains closed

### Weeks 1-2 (where we are now)

The initial shock is being absorbed. Middle Eastern spot allocations are fully consumed or cancelled. Korean crackers are announcing shutdowns or rate reductions one by one. Chinese producers are still quoting normally into flat domestic demand, and FOB prices from China have not yet spiked — but they are firming. Buyers who act now are still buying at near-normal China-origin pricing. The window is narrowing as more procurement teams reach the same conclusion simultaneously.

### Weeks 4-8

Demand concentration into China-origin supply becomes fully visible in pricing. FOB China prices for commodity PP and PE are likely to increase 5-12% as the entire region competes for the same supply pool. Freight surcharges compound — expect cumulative surcharge additions of $100-200/TEU on China-SE Asia routes. Producers begin to manage export allocations more tightly, prioritizing committed buyers over spot purchasers. Buyers without supply agreements in place face allocation risk, not just price risk.

### Months 3-6

If the closure persists beyond 90 days, second-order effects dominate. Chinese strategic reserves begin to draw down meaningfully, and naphtha rationing to coastal crackers intensifies. CTO/MTO-origin grades command a structural premium over naphtha-based Chinese grades. Some producers may restrict export volumes to ensure domestic supply adequacy. Southeast Asian converters face real production risk — not just margin compression, but inability to procure feedstock at any price from non-China sources. Buyers with term contracts and committed volumes are insulated. Spot buyers face the worst of both price escalation and allocation uncertainty.

## What to watch

Three indicators that matter for the next 30-60 days:

1. **Naphtha pricing in Northeast Asia** — If C&F Japan naphtha breaks above $750/MT, expect further Korean and Singaporean cracker shutdowns
2. **Chinese cracker operating rates** — Sinopec has already begun rationing naphtha to crackers in favor of fuel production. Watch for Fujian Gulei and Zhenhai rate reductions
3. **Freight surcharges** — CMA CGM's Emergency Fuel Surcharge ($75/TEU intra-Asia) is the first wave. More carriers will follow

## Practical buyer actions

The following actions are relevant for mid-tier distributors and converters in Vietnam and the Philippines sourcing commodity PP, PE, and LDPE:

1. **Secure China-origin supply commitments now.** Shift from spot purchasing to term arrangements where possible. The current pricing window — where China-origin FOB has not yet fully reflected demand concentration — will not remain open indefinitely.

2. **Request firm CFR quotes before surcharges escalate further.** Freight surcharges are additive and tend to increase in waves as carriers follow each other. Lock CFR pricing where your supplier can offer it. If freight is uncertain, accept FOB quotes and manage freight separately — but get the product price locked.

3. **Review inventory positions and build buffer stock.** The cost of carrying 60 days of inventory at current financing rates is substantially lower than the cost of a supply gap or a 10-15% price spike. If cash flow allows, build inventory now while pricing is still near baseline.

4. **Evaluate alternative grades for critical applications.** If your production depends on specific Middle Eastern or Korean grades, identify Chinese equivalents and begin qualification testing immediately. Grade switching takes time — starting the process now means having options if the disruption extends beyond Q2.

5. **Diversify supplier relationships within China.** Not all Chinese supply is equal. Coastal naphtha-based producers face the same feedstock squeeze as Korean facilities (at a delay). CTO/MTO-origin product from inland producers carries less supply risk. Ask your supplier about feedstock origin.

6. **Monitor freight rates weekly.** Intra-Asia freight is changing faster than polymer prices. A freight increase of $100-200/TEU on a 22-MT container adds $4.50-9.00/MT to delivered cost — enough to change the economics of a marginal order.

## The structural question

Every producing region is running on reserves. The difference is buffer length. Korea's buffer has been consumed. Singapore's facilities are going dark. China has the largest buffer — but it isn't infinite.

The buyers who are positioned well right now are the ones who secured China-origin supply before the corridor narrowing became consensus. For everyone else, the question isn't whether to pivot to Chinese supply — it's whether competitive allocations are still available.
