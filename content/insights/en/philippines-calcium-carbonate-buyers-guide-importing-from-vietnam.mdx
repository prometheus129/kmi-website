> **In short:** The Philippine market for industrial calcium carbonate is import-led at the quality tier plastics converters use: about **61,000 t of HS 2836.50 imports in 2023**, with **Vietnam the #1 origin (~26,500 t)**, ahead of China and Malaysia. Duty is a non-issue if the paperwork is right — **0% under ATIGA with a Form D** (MFN is only 3%) — and Haiphong→Manila is a short direct lane at **3–5 days**. This guide covers the classification, the Form D mechanics, a worked landed-cost example, and how to pick a grade for pipe, film, sacks, and masterbatch.

## The Philippine Supply Picture: Local Millers and Imports

Two facts frame the market, and they point in the same direction.

First, the Philippines is not without domestic calcium carbonate. Limestone is quarried at enormous scale (mostly for cement and flux), and a small cluster of millers — concentrated in Bulacan — grinds industrial CaCO₃ powder for construction, agriculture, feed, and mid-range industrial uses. Published domestic grades, however, typically top out around **325–400 mesh** (roughly a 38–44 µm top size), uncoated, with little or no published whiteness data.

Second, the tier that plastics compounding, premium paint, and white masterbatch actually consume — **fine (sub-10 µm top cut), high-whiteness, stearic-coated GCC** — comes in by sea. No domestic miller publishes grades in this tier. In 2023 the Philippines imported roughly **61,000 t of calcium carbonate under HS 2836.50** (about US$14.3M — the heading spans all grades), led by **Vietnam (~26,500 t)** and **China (~22,300 t)**, with Malaysia a distant third (World Bank WITS / UN Comtrade). Philippine exports of the same heading were negligible in 2023 — the trade runs one way.

So if you are buying fine filler grade in the Philippines today, you are almost certainly buying imported material — most often Vietnamese — whether you import it yourself or buy it through a distributor's warehouse with a margin on top. The purpose of this guide is to show what buying it direct actually involves.

## Classification: HS 2836.50, and Why the Masterbatch Line Is Different

Raw calcium carbonate powder — coated or uncoated — is classified under **HS 2836.50**. The Philippine tariff schedule splits it into two national lines, **2836.50.10** (food or pharmaceutical grade) and **2836.50.90** (other, which covers industrial GCC), but the rates are identical, so the split does not change your duty bill.

**Filler masterbatch is a different product in a different chapter**: CaCO₃ already compounded into a plastic carrier is a chemical preparation under **HS 3824.99**. If you buy finished masterbatch rather than powder, both the duty treatment and the supplier universe change. Keep the two separate in your entries — misclassification is one of the few ways to turn a 0% shipment into a customs problem.

## Duty: What the Tariff Columns Actually Say

Verified against the **Philippine Tariff Commission's Tariff Finder** (AHTN 2022 schedule, July 2026), both national lines of 2836.50 carry:

| Regime | Rate | Origin document |
|---|---|---|
| MFN (no preference claimed) | **3%** | none |
| ATIGA (ASEAN origin, incl. Vietnam) | **0%** | Form D |
| ACFTA (China origin) | **0%** | Form E |
| RCEP | **0%** | RCEP proof of origin |

Two observations follow from that table.

**Origin paperwork is worth exactly 3 percentage points.** The MFN rate is already low, so the Form D is a real but modest saving — on a US$6,700 CIF container, about US$200. It is still a costless saving for one document, but nobody should build an import case on tariff arithmetic alone.

**Vietnam has no tariff edge over China into the Philippines.** Both land at 0% with the right certificate. Vietnam's lead is not duty. The plausible drivers are spec and price: the premium northern-Vietnam (Yên Bái) calcite tier publishes **97–98% whiteness** at competitive FOB levels, with the short Haiphong→Manila lane a secondary factor. When you compare origins, compare TDS numbers and delivered price, not tariff columns.

12% VAT applies on the duty-inclusive landed value regardless of origin — for a VAT-registered manufacturer this is creditable input tax, i.e., cash flow rather than cost.

## Importing from Vietnam, Step by Step

For a commercial shipment, the sequence is below. As a reference point, goods with an FOB value of PHP 50,000 or more are formal entries by definition.

1. **Contract on clear specs.** Grade name plus the numbers that define it: D97/D50, whiteness *with its measurement standard*, purity, coating level. Require a per-lot Certificate of Analysis against the TDS.
2. **Require the Form D.** The exporter obtains it in Vietnam (paper or e-Form D via the ASEAN Single Window). The origin rule for this line is regional value content ≥40% or change of tariff heading — Vietnamese quarried-and-ground limestone qualifies comfortably. The certificate must match the invoice and B/L details; discrepancies are the most common reason a preference claim fails.
3. **Book the freight.** Direct Haiphong→Manila services run weekly with roughly **3–5 day** transit. GCC is heavy cargo: a 20-ft container weighs out around **25 t** before it cubes out, so per-ton freight is low.
4. **Lodge the entry.** Your licensed customs broker files the goods declaration with the Bureau of Customs, claiming the ATIGA preference against the Form D. Budget for VAT (12%, creditable), brokerage, port charges, and trucking; BOC's fixed fees (documentary stamp, import processing) are minor line items.
5. **Check the COA against the TDS before the container ships**, not after it lands. Whiteness and top-cut disputes are much cheaper to resolve at origin.

## Worked Example: Haiphong → Manila Landed Cost

Illustrative numbers, stated as assumptions — not a quotation. One 20-ft container, ~25 t of coated fine GCC at an assumed **US$250/t FOB Haiphong** — a figure chosen for round arithmetic, not a price signal; actual FOB varies with grade, coating, volume, and market:

| Component | Amount (US$) | Per ton (US$) |
|---|---|---|
| FOB value (25 t × 250) | 6,250 | 250.00 |
| Ocean freight, HPH→MNL all-in (indicative 300–500; midpoint) | 400 | 16.00 |
| Marine insurance (~0.3%) | 20 | 0.80 |
| **CIF Manila** | **6,670** | **266.80** |
| Duty with Form D (ATIGA 0%) | 0 | 0.00 |
| Brokerage, port charges, trucking (illustrative) | 600 | 24.00 |
| **Landed cost, ex-VAT** | **7,270** | **≈291** |

Without a Form D, add 3% duty on CIF (≈US$200, or US$8/t) — landed cost rises to ≈US$299/t. VAT of 12% applies on the customs value plus duty and certain other charges (≈US$800 on the CIF-plus-duty base here; your broker's exact computation adds minor fees) and is recovered as input tax by VAT-registered buyers.

Three things to take from the table rather than the exact numbers: **ocean freight is a small line** (US$12–20/t on this lane), **the duty is zero if one document is in order**, and **the FOB price and the grade dominate the outcome** — logistics costs are broadly fixed for everyone on this lane. A US$20/t difference in FOB matters more than anything you can realistically negotiate on freight or local charges. Freight and local-charge figures are forwarder-indicative as of mid-2026 — confirm current rates before committing.

## Choosing a Grade: Pipe, Film, Sacks, Masterbatch, Paint

Ignore mesh labels where you can — below ~400 mesh, physical sieving no longer applies and "1250 mesh" is a marketing shorthand. Read grades on **D50** (median particle size) and **D97** (top cut — the main cause of film defects and screen blockage), plus whiteness, purity, and coating.

| Application | Typical D50 | What matters most |
|---|---|---|
| Woven sacks / general filler masterbatch | ~2–3 µm | cost per ton, consistent top cut, coating for dispersion |
| Blown film | ~1–2 µm | fine top cut (D97), stearic coating, whiteness for white film |
| PVC pipe / profile / fittings | ~1–5 µm | purity, coating at higher loadings, whiteness on visible product |
| Paint / coatings | grade-dependent | whiteness, oil absorption, top cut |

## Whiteness and the TiO₂ Question

For white product — white film, appliance housings, visible pipe and profile — whiteness is not cosmetic; it is money. Titanium dioxide is one of the most expensive ingredients in a white formulation, and fine, high-whiteness GCC lets you cut part of it: industry trade coverage (Plastics Technology) puts fine GCC at **60–90% cheaper than TiO₂** per ton, with around **15% total cost savings** reported in white film masterbatch when part of the TiO₂ load is displaced. CaCO₃ does not replace TiO₂'s opacity one-for-one — it is a partial substitution — but in formulations where the whiteness target (rather than opacity) is the binding constraint, a 97–98%-whiteness grade (as published — the same same-standard comparison discipline applies here) gives a meaningfully wider substitution window than a duller one.

One discipline when comparing grades on whiteness: **check the measurement standard, not just the number.** "Whiteness" (broad-spectrum) and "brightness" (R457, single-wavelength) are different scales, and suppliers do not always say which one they are quoting. Ask for the standard on the TDS and compare like with like.

## MOQ, Lead Times, and Practicalities

- **MOQ:** one 20-ft container (~25 t) is the practical floor for direct import economics; smaller volumes belong with a local distributor.
- **Lead time:** with material in stock at origin, think in terms of days, not months — 3–5 days on the water plus booking and clearance on both ends; a few weeks door-to-door is a realistic planning figure.
- **Packaging:** 25 kg bags on pallets or ~1 t jumbo bags; jumbo bags suit compounders with bulk handling, small bags suit resale and manual dosing.
- **Payment:** typical first-order terms in this trade are a deposit with balance against shipping documents; agree terms alongside specs, not after.

## Buying Direct vs Through a Distributor

A distributor earns its margin when you need small lots, credit, or local warehousing. Direct import earns its keep at one-container-and-up volumes — the math above shows what direct costs: at ≈US$291/t landed, any margin layered between the Vietnamese plant and your line is visible in your cost sheet. The practical blockers to going direct are not tariffs or freight; they are spec confidence (will lot 14 match lot 1?), paperwork discipline (Form D, COA), and having someone accountable on the origin side.
